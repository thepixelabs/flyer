      Building the blend, one round at a time

        For the curious: the exact rules
        • Strength of each input. Sugar sensors are driven at 80 pulses a second the whole time. Each added taste or smell drives its sensor cells at 30.
        • Repeats. The first screen runs every test 16 times. Checking again uses 64, each combining round 48 and the final check 32, each time with fresh random noise.
        • Noise. Every number has a ± value: the standard error, roughly how far the number would move if the whole thing were run again. A result “clearly helps” when it is more than twice its noise.
        • Shortlist. Up to ten channels whose screen gain is bigger than its noise are checked again. Only the ones above twice their noise go on to the rounds.
        • Rounds. Each round runs the blend so far next to the blend plus each candidate, under the same conditions. The best one joins if its gain is more than twice the noise. Otherwise the search stops.

        What is data, and what is the image model

        It is a portrait of the fly brain's response to its favourite flavour: the cells that fired while it tasted the blend, where they sit, and how they are wired together. It is not a picture of food, and not something the fly sees or imagines. In this wiring the feeding circuit is almost cut off from vision, so there is no picture in its head to show.

        From the brain data

        • Which cells fired (these of 138,639)
        • Where each one sits, seen from the front or from any angle you turn the brain to
        • Which are connected, from the FlyWire wiring map
        • How often each fired: the size of the dot or circle
        • When each first fired: the colour, in the circuit view
        • The outline of the brain, from all its cells
        • The shape of the brain in depth: the depth map of all 138,639 neurons
        • The colours, if you choose colours from the data: they show when each cell first fired

        From the image model

        • The medium: ink, paper, brush or blueprint blue (and the colours, if you choose ink colours)
        • Texture, shading and the feel of the line
        • Extra strokes in the free version that aren't in the data (strict removes them)
        • Nothing about where things go. The words only name a style; the shapes come from the line map and the depth map
        The seven steps from brain to drawing

        1. 1. The cells that fired

          These cells fired while the fly tasted its favourite blend. Each dot sits where that cell really is. A bigger dot fired more often. The colour shows when it first fired, from 0 to 60 ms and later: the signal starts at the mouth and spreads.

        2. 2. The wires between them

          These connections (5 or more synapses, the contact points between cells) join cells that both fired. The picture draws the 3,000 strongest as curves from one cell to the other. The curve is a drawing choice: real wiring follows the cells' branches, which the map doesn't trace.

        3. 3. The line map

          Only the structure is kept: the brain's outline, the 900 strongest connections (15 or more synapses) and a circle for each cell, white on black. This and the depth map are the only pictures the image model is given.

        4. 4. The depth map

          All 138,639 neurons at their real 3D positions, nearer ones brighter. It gives the drawing the real rounded shape of the brain, so shading follows the actual anatomy instead of whatever the model imagines.

        5. 5. The image model paints it

          An image model is a program that learned to make pictures from many examples. It cleans up speckle step by step into a picture. ControlNet holds its strokes to the line map and its shading to the depth map, and the model paints the style. The words only name a style, never food or a fly, so where the lines go has to come from the data.

          With ink colours it starts from pure random speckle and the colours come from the style. With colours from the data it starts from the coloured circuit picture instead, so the colours keep showing when each cell first fired.

          You can turn the 3D brain and press Draw this view: the line map and depth map are made again from that angle, from the same real positions.

        6. 6. Strict and free

          Free is everything the model drew, flourishes included. Strict keeps its ink only near real data lines and fades the rest to the colour of the paper.

        7. 7. The honesty score

          We find the drawing's strongest lines and check how many lie within 4 pixels of a data line (on data), and how much of the data they trace (covers). Chance is what random lines would score, because part of the canvas is already close to some data line. Strict scores high partly by design, since it erases ink away from the data, so look at its covers score.

        A fly's brain, asked a simple question

        This page runs a copy of a real fruit fly's brain on this computer and asks it one question: of all the things a fly can taste and smell, which ones make it most eager to eat?

        Nobody gives the brain a menu or tells it what flies like. The answer comes only from how its nerve cells are wired together. At the end, the brain's response is turned into a drawing.

        You can watch it happen live, turn the brain around in 3D, and see where every number comes from.

        The brain comes from FlyWire

        In 2024 a large team of scientists published FlyWire: a map of every nerve cell in the brain of one adult fruit fly, and of how those cells connect. They built it from very detailed microscope images of the brain, with computers doing much of the tracing and people checking and correcting it.

        The map has about 139,000 neurons (nerve cells) and millions of connections between them.

        The glowing cloud on this page is that brain. Every dot is one of its neurons, placed where it really sits. The two big wings on the sides are the optic lobes, which handle vision. The taste cells come in at the bottom middle, near the mouth.

        From a wiring map to a working brain

        A wiring map on its own does nothing, much like a road map has no traffic. To bring it to life we treat every neuron as a simple unit that collects signals from the neurons wired into it.

        When enough signals arrive in a short time, the neuron fires: it sends a brief pulse (a spike) to every neuron it connects to. Some connections push the next neuron toward firing, others hold it back.

        That rule is all we add. Who signals whom, and how strongly, comes straight from the map. This kind of model comes from a study by Shiu and colleagues (Nature, 2024), who used it to study how a fly decides to feed. Here it runs on this computer's graphics card, often with more than a thousand copies of the brain at once.

        How we ask it

        When a hungry fly tastes something good, it reaches out its proboscis (the little trunk it eats with). One neuron, called MN9, drives that movement. The more often MN9 fires, the stronger the urge to eat. MN9 is our meter.

        Sugar is always being tasted during every test, because in this model nothing else starts feeding on its own. Then we add every other taste and smell the fly has, one at a time, by switching on the sensor cells for it, and watch whether MN9 fires more or less than with sugar alone.

        Each test lasts one simulated second and is repeated many times with a little random noise, a bit like asking many flies instead of one. The wall of small brains shows these tests as they happen.

        Finding the favourite

        The search works like a careful tasting competition, in four steps:

        1. Try everything. All 63 other tastes and smells are tried on top of sugar, side by side.
        2. Check again. The ones that seemed to help are tested again, many more times, so luck can't sneak through.
        3. Build a blend. The best one joins the blend. Then the rest are tried on top of that blend, one round at a time. Something only joins if it clearly helps.
        4. Confirm. Plain sugar and the final blend are run again from scratch. That difference is the answer you see.

        What it found

        We ran the whole search six times, each time with different random noise. Every time, the brain picked the same kind of blend: sugar plus water, plus low salt, plus one group of taste cells in the throat. What those throat cells detect in real flies isn't known yet.

        That blend made the feeding neuron fire about 12 to 16 more times a second than sugar alone.

        No smell was ever chosen: most smells made the brain less eager to eat, and none clearly helped. Bitter made it much less eager. Real flies like a little salt too, which is a good sign the model behaves sensibly.

        How the picture is made

        Once the answer is confirmed, we look at the final test of the favourite blend: which neurons fired, where they sit, and which of them are wired to each other. That gives a map of dots and lines.

        An image model (a program that learned to make pictures, also running on this computer) gets two pictures and a few words. The first picture is that line map. The second is a depth map: all 138,639 neurons at their real 3D positions, nearer ones brighter, so the drawing takes the real rounded shape of the brain. The words only name a style, like “ink drawing on old paper”, and never mention food or flies. So the shapes come from the brain, and the model adds the look.

        You choose the colours. Ink colours come from the style. Colours from the data start the drawing from the coloured circuit, so its colours still show when each cell first fired.

        You can also turn the 3D brain to any angle and press Draw this view. Both maps are made again from that angle, from the same real positions, and the same experiment is drawn without running it again.

        Only some image models are offered. Each one was tested by drawing the same saved experiment, and only the ones that stayed faithful to the data lines made the list.

        The result is a portrait of the brain's response. It is not food, and it is not something the fly sees or imagines. The fly's feeding circuit is almost cut off from its vision, so there is no picture in its head to show. Next to every drawing we score how much of it sits on the real data.

        What this can't tell us

        • It is a model. Real neurons are far more complicated than the simple units used here, so this is a careful guess about how the wiring behaves, not a recording from a living fly.
        • One readout. MN9 is one sign of wanting to eat, not the whole story of appetite.
        • Same strength for everything. Each taste and smell is switched on at the same strength, but real foods and smells differ a lot.
        • One fly. The map comes from a single fly's brain.
        • The style is the model's. The look of the drawing, and in the free version some extra strokes, come from the image model. The strict version and the score show which parts are data.
        For the curious: one change we made to the published model

        In the original model, some cells in the smell centre that should calm activity down act as if they excite it, so switching on a single smell sensor set off around 10,000 neurons. We made those cells calming unless they are known to be exciting, and gave slow chemical messengers (dopamine, serotonin, octopamine) no fast effect. Smells then reach a few hundred to a couple of thousand neurons, and taste works as before.
